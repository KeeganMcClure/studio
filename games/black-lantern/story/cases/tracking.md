## The truth (what is really going on)

- **The club.** **Club Satin**, Fourth Street, Reno. A topless bar with a dead marquee, closed by the city in March 1996 after a fire inspection nobody believes was about fire. Its owner, **Royce Kettering**, ran the stage, the books and the back rooms. What happened in the back rooms stays off screen: the case says only that girls owed him money and paid it back.
- **The dancer.** **Darlene Fisk**, stage name **VESPER**, 26 in 1996. The best dancer Club Satin ever had, and by its last winter dying of what she was using: thin, sores on her arms and back, still the best. She wanted one thing more than getting clean: to keep dancing.
- **The deal (arc touch).** In February 1996 a tall, courteous man in an old deacon's coat came to a matinee, sat at the back, and asked her afterward what she wanted most. She signed his ledger for "one more year of dancing, and then another." She's an **account**. This never gets explained in play; it shows only as the photo below and the page.
- **The tape.** Kettering didn't trust the deal; he wanted a cut. He'd read things. He filmed Vesper's last show on the club's security deck and **chanted over it from behind the camera**, a working he'd pieced together from a book he bought at a swap meet: a **calling of the Audience**, the nameless thing that lives in the attention of people watching. The tape is the working. Every time someone watches it, the Audience watches back through them, and Vesper gets one more night of dancing out of their attention. That's how the deal is paid: not by the ledger's holder, by viewers.
- **What watching does.** The viewer comes away *known*: they feel the Audience behind their eyes, sure that everyone can see what they've done. **"WE KNOW"** is what the Audience says, in Kettering's handwriting on the original label and in the viewer's own handwriting afterward. Watchers cover their windows (to stop being seen), stop sleeping, write the words on everything, and, given time, hurt themselves trying to get the eyes out (self-harm stays off screen: a bandage, a closed door, a line). The clerk, **Wes Pratt**, is a week into it.
- **What Vesper is now.** She never died. She still dances, every night, in the closed club, for the Audience, with the sores and the speed and the same face she had in 1996. Each viewing keeps her going. She's not a villain; she's the thing the deal and the tape turned her into, and somewhere in her is Darlene, who wants to stop. The photo "taken last month" is a city inspector's flash photo of the club's dark dance floor, with her in it.
- **The copies.** Kettering dubbed **five copies** in the club's basement tape room (the **dub room**) the night before the city closed it, and sold them through pawn shops to spread the working: more viewers, more nights. Over thirty years they moved hand to hand. The trail is those five, plus the master in the dub room.
- **Who else is watching (the soft clock).** **Kettering** is still alive, 74, living in a weekly motel on Fourth Street under another name ("Mr. Royce"), and he's buying the copies back, because the Audience is tired of the same few viewers and he needs the tape to travel again. He's the "someone else" watching the copies move. If he gets them before the agents, he mails them out.
- **Rules (internal logic).**
  1. Watching a copy feeds the Audience and gives Vesper one more night; it also shows the watcher something true (a clue) the Audience lets slip. Each watch is one step of the tracking lines.
  2. The Audience can only see through people who've watched. A party that never watches is invisible to it (roamers in the club are blind to non-watchers past a few tiles; see roamers).
  3. A copy destroyed with a magnet (degaussed) or burned is one less door for the Audience. Each destroyed copy weakens the boss; a copy kept is a heal.
  4. The master in the dub room ends it: erase the master while Vesper is held in the light and the working ends. Kettering's chant, said backward from his own notebook, unties Darlene from the Audience (the ledger's claim is not the case's business).
  5. Bright, steady light hurts the Audience; flicker feeds it. The club's old stage lights, if Computers wakes them, are a weapon and a shortcut.
